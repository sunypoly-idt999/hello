My reading is that the three most important unanswered questions are:

1. **What ultimately makes standing legitimate when different bases conflict?**  
   If authority, expertise, affectedness, responsibility, and representation point in different directions, how should they be weighed? Who decides whose claim has priority—and on what grounds?

2. **When is silence a legitimate decision, and when is it a failure to recognize standing?**  
   What response is owed to someone who raises a consequential claim? How quickly must they be answered, and what must a valid response contain? Without such norms, “no” and “nobody has decided” become indistinguishable.

3. **How can standing be made operational rather than ceremonial?**  
   What concrete practices allow a project to detect that formal participation is not producing real influence, then repair the situation? This includes deciding when boundaries, success criteria, authority, or even the project’s meaningful purpose must be renegotiated.

Together, these ask whether standing is merely a useful interpretation of project failure or a genuine governing principle:

> Who counts, why do they count, what response are they owed, and how can the project prove that their standing has had real consequences?
