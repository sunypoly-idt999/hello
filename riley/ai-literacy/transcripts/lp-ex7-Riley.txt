Here's HAAII × UX (Human and AI Interaction × UX Design) explained at three levels:

## For a curious 10-year-old

You know how when you use an app, someone had to decide where the buttons go and what color they should be, so it's easy for you to use? That's called UX — it stands for "user experience," and it's basically making sure technology is easy and pleasant for people to use.

Now imagine some of that technology has AI in it — like a chatbot, or an app that finishes your sentences, or a robot vacuum that learns your house. HAAII is the study of how people and AI *work together*. It asks questions like: How do you know if the robot is doing a good job? What happens if the AI makes a mistake — does it tell you, or just guess quietly? Should the AI ask you before it does something big?

So HAAII × UX is people who design AI stuff thinking really hard about how it *feels* to use — so the AI feels helpful and trustworthy, not creepy or confusing.

## For a first-year college student

UX (user experience) design is the practice of shaping how people interact with a product — the flow, the interface, the feel of using it. Traditionally, UX design has centered around fairly predictable systems: you tap a button, something predictable happens.

AI breaks that predictability. AI systems are probabilistic, they can behave differently each time, and they can act with a degree of autonomy — generating content, making recommendations, or taking actions on a user's behalf. That changes what "good design" even means.

HAAII (Human and AI Interaction) is the subfield that studies this shift. It draws from HCI (Human-Computer Interaction) but adds new concerns specific to AI systems, such as:

- **Trust and transparency** — does the user understand *why* the AI did what it did?
- **Explainability** — can the system's reasoning be surfaced in a way a non-expert understands?
- **Error handling** — how should a system communicate uncertainty or failure gracefully?
- **Agency and control** — how much should the AI do autonomously vs. defer to the human?
- **Mental models** — how do users' expectations of AI (often shaped by sci-fi or hype) diverge from what the system can actually do, and how does design correct for that?

So HAAII × UX is essentially UX design methodology adapted for a world where the "system" you're designing for is no longer fully deterministic — it's a collaborator with its own behavior.

## For a working UX professional

At a practitioner level, HAAII × UX represents a shift in the underlying design paradigm: from designing static interfaces to designing for dynamic, adaptive, probabilistic systems. A few of the core tensions this raises:

- **From flows to negotiations.** Traditional UX maps a user journey with defined states. AI-driven experiences are closer to an ongoing negotiation between user intent and system interpretation — prompting, clarification, and correction become core interaction patterns, not edge cases.
- **Calibrated trust, not maximized trust.** A recurring finding in HAAII research is that over-trust is as dangerous as under-trust. Design work increasingly involves calibrating a user's confidence in the system to match its actual reliability — through affordances like confidence indicators, provenance/citations, or friction at high-stakes moments.
- **Explainability as a design material.** XAI (explainable AI) isn't just a backend concern; it's becoming a UX pattern language of its own — progressive disclosure of reasoning, counterfactuals ("what if I changed X"), and surfacing model limitations contextually rather than in a disclaimer buried in settings.
- **Evaluating AI-driven UX.** Classic usability metrics (task completion, time-on-task) don't fully capture things like perceived agency, appropriate reliance, or trust repair after an error. Researchers are adapting mixed-method approaches — behavioral logging plus qualitative trust interviews — to evaluate these systems.
- **Human-in-the-loop and autonomy gradients.** Practitioners are moving away from binary "AI does it" vs. "human does it" toward designing explicit autonomy levels, similar to how autonomous vehicles are classified — and giving users legible control over where on that spectrum a given interaction sits.

## HAAII × UX — one solid explanation

**UX (User Experience) design** is the practice of shaping how people interact with a product — the interface, the flow, the overall experience of using it.

**HAAII (Human and AI Interaction)** is a subfield that studies how people interact specifically with AI systems, as distinct from regular software. It draws from HCI (Human-Computer Interaction) but focuses on what changes when the system you're interacting with is probabilistic and semi-autonomous rather than fixed and rule-based.

Here are the core concepts, defined directly:

- **Trust calibration**: The goal isn't to make users trust an AI system as much as possible — it's to make their trust *accurate*. If a user trusts a system more than its actual reliability warrants, that's over-trust, and it leads to bad outcomes when the system fails. Design work here includes confidence indicators, source citations, and friction points at high-stakes decisions.

- **Explainability (XAI)**: The practice of surfacing *why* an AI system produced a given output, in terms a non-technical user can understand. This includes things like showing reasoning steps, offering counterfactuals ("here's what would change if you adjusted X"), or contextually flagging the system's limitations rather than burying them in a settings page.

- **Error handling for probabilistic systems**: Traditional software fails in predictable, repeatable ways. AI systems fail unpredictably and often confidently — they don't always know when they're wrong. UX work here focuses on designing graceful ways for a system to express uncertainty, and for users to catch and correct errors easily.

- **Autonomy gradients**: Instead of a binary "AI does it" vs. "human does it," modern AI UX design defines explicit levels of autonomy — similar to the classification levels used for self-driving cars — and gives users legible control over where a given interaction sits on that spectrum.

- **Evaluation methods**: Standard usability metrics (task completion rate, time-on-task) don't capture things unique to AI interaction, like perceived agency or trust repair after a system error. Researchers combine behavioral data (logs, completion rates) with qualitative methods (trust interviews, think-aloud studies) to evaluate these systems more completely.

So HAAII × UX, put simply: it's the design and research discipline focused on making AI systems that are honest about their own uncertainty, correctable when wrong, and appropriately — not maximally — trusted.
