1\. Main Arguments

**Lu et al. (2024)** argues that AI assistance for UX practitioners remains too technology-driven and insufficiently human-centered. Through a systematic literature review of 359 papers organized by the Double Diamond design framework (Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver), the authors find that only 24.3% of reviewed papers engaged with human-centered methodologies or user needs, and that research disproportionately clusters in the Develop and Deliver phases. Their central claim is that AI-for-UX tools often overlook empathy-building — the core mindset of UX practice — and treat UI screens as isolated artifacts rather than as parts of a connected user experience.

5\. Synthesis: What the Two Readings Together Suggest
-----------------------------------------------------

Read together, these two papers suggest that "human-centered AI for UX" is not a single, settled problem but operates at two different levels that need to connect.

Amudavalli's paper operates at the level of a single interaction: given one AI-driven feature (a recommendation), how should its reasoning be surfaced to a user in the interface? Lu et al.'s paper operates at the level of the entire UX practice and research ecosystem: not just how one AI output should be explained to an end user, but whether and how AI should assist the _designer_ across the full arc of discovering problems, defining them, developing solutions, and delivering evaluated designs. Their critique is less about individual interface patterns and more about whether AI tools understand — and are trained on data that reflects — the underlying human process of empathy-building and iterative design.

Taken together, the two papers suggest that "interpretability" in Amudavalli's narrow sense (explaining a single AI decision to an end user) is one small piece of a much larger challenge that Lu et al. describe: building AI tools that respect and support human interpretive, empathetic work throughout an entire design and decision-making process — not just at the moment of final output. Amudavalli's optimistic, feature-level findings and Lu et al.'s cautious, systemic findings are not incompatible; they may simply be describing different scales of the same underlying problem — one a promising local intervention, the other a reminder that local interventions do not by themselves resolve field-wide, structural gaps between AI capability and human-centered design practice.
